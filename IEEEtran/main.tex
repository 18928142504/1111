\begin{abstract}
Optimizing virtual machine (VM) scheduling is paramount for enhancing energy efficiency and resource utilization in dynamic cloud data centers. However, aggressive VM allocation often exacerbates resource contention, leading to critical Service Level Agreement (SLA) violations. Existing models frequently overlook this delicate balance, prioritizing energy reduction while neglecting SLA integrity, and often rely on suboptimal centralized allocation schemes. To address these challenges, we propose BAK-SCSO-VMS in cloud data centers, a novel BiTCN-ATTENTION-KAN (BAK) based prediction and Sand Cat Swarm Optimization (SCSO) driven VM scheduling framework. BAK-SCSO-VMS integrates the BAK neural network model to accurately predict VM energy consumption. By combining Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Networks (BiTCN) with an attention-based Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN), this model effectively captures multi-variable correlations, temporal dependencies, and complex non-linear relationships through flexible, learnable curve fitting. Building upon these predictions, a dynamic VM scheduling algorithm powered by SCSO is designed to intelligently explore optimal VM configurations that balance energy saving with SLA adherence. The experimental results demonstrate that BAK-SCSO-VMS achieves significant energy efficiency gains from 16.1\% to 21.9\% over the state-of-art baselines, while restricting the SLA violation rate to a mere 0.0165\%. Furthermore, our scheme exhibit superior adaptability and robust generalization performance across diverse VM scales.

\end{abstract}

\section{introduction}
% no \IEEEPARstart
With the upgrading of the global digital industry and the large-scale development of artificial intelligence, both the number and scale of data centers have witnessed explosive expansion.  According to a report by the International Energy Agency (IEA), global energy consumption accounted for about 2\% of total electricity consumption in 2022, approximately 460 TWh \cite{zeng2022adaptive}. It is speculated that this figure may increase to around 1,000 TWh by 2026 and will continue to rise in the foreseeable future. Energy consumption and related carbon emissions have become the main obstacles hindering the sustainable development of data centers. Consequently, implementing robust energy management strategies that reconcile energy reduction with high-performance service delivery has become an imperative research priority.

To mitigate excessive energy consumption in cloud data centers, virtualization-enabled resource management have been extensively explored by both academia and industry. By leveraging virtualization, a single Physical machine (PM) can concurrently support multiple Virtual Machine (VM) instances \cite{mhedheb2013load}, significantly enhancing hardware utilization compared to traditional one-to-one mapping architectures. Dynamic VM scheduling serves as a critical  strategy that intelligently allocates VMs to PMs based on changing resource demands. These scheduling decisions are typically implemented using live migration technology, which allows underutilized PMs to be transitioned to a sleep or powered-off state. This minimizes idle power consumption and optimizes the data center’s overall energy usage.

Despite these advancements, contemporary VM scheduling technologies remain suboptimal. The Service Level Agreement (SLA) defines critical performance benchmarks—such as bandwidth guarantees and latency thresholds—that service providers must uphold. An escalation in SLA violation rates not only degrades user experience but also exposes providers to substantial financial penalties and reputational damage. First, most existing scheduling frameworks are reactive \cite{yang2024dual}. They initiate VM schedulings only after performance bottlenecks are detected, by which time the SLA has frequently been breached. Second, conventional models often focus exclusively on CPU utilization while neglecting memory dynamics. This narrow focus can lead to deceptive results where low CPU usage masks high memory swap rates, ultimately causing system performance to collapse. Third, while previous deep learning models, such as Temporal Convolutional networks (TCN) \cite{li2023tcn}, exhibit parallel computing advantages in capturing local features, they typically rely on fixed convolution kernels for the final output. This architectural constraint prevents them from accurately predicting sudden, non-linear workload spikes, resulting in significant underestimation during dynamic fluctuations..

To address the aforementioned limitations, we propose a proactive energy-SLA balanced framework called BAK-SCSO-VMS, which integrates advanced deep learning for proactive prediction with a robust swarm intelligence algorithm for VM scheduling. Specifically, we develop the BiTCN-ATTENTION-KAN (BAK) model to accurately predict  VM energy consumption, such as CPU utilization, and RAM utilization. While the Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Network (BiTCN) excels at capturing long-term temporal dependencies from historical data. To overcome this, we incorporate the Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) with an attention mechanism. By utilizing learnable, non-linear activation functions on edges, KAN provides an arbitrarily flexible curve-fitting capability. This allows the model to capture both subtle stabilities and sharp, non-linear fluctuations in VM energy consumption, facilitating proactive resource management timely transitioning idle PMs into sleep mode.

Furthermore, we employ the Sand Cat Swarm Optimization (SCSO) algorithm to determine the optimal VM scheduling. To enhance its search efficiency, a Lens Imaging Learning (LIL) mechanism is integrated into the SCSO algorithm. The LIL mechanism effectively prevents the population from being trapped in local optima by diversifying the search space, thereby ensuring the discovery of a global optimal solution for energy-SLA balancing. The integration and allocation enables a more efficient, adaptive, and proactive approach for cloud resource optimization. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item  We design the BAK-SCSO-VMS framework, a proactive Energy-SLA balanced framework for VM scheduling in cloud data centers.

    \item  BAK-SCSO-VMS first integrates the Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Network (BiTCN) and the Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) to effectively capture long-term temporal dependencies and multivariate correlations for VM energy consumption prediction. Furthermore, the framework incorporates Sand Cat Swarm Optimization (SCSO) for optimal VM scheduling.

    \item  Through extensive simulations using the Cloudsim toolkit, we demonstrate the superiority of the proposed BAK-SCSO-VMS resource. The experimental results reveal that BAK-SCSO-VMS achieves an energy efficiency improvement from 16.1\% to 21.9\% compared to the most advanced algorithms, respectively, while maintaining a remarkably low SLA violation rate of 0.0165\%, proving its robust generalization across diverse VM scales.
\end{itemize}

Section V reports the performance evaluation results. Finally, Section VII concludes the paper and outlines directions for future research.

\section{related work}
\subsection{Research on VM Energy Consumption Prediction}

The accelerated advancement in energy consumption of data centers not only brings high operational costs to service providers but also exacerbates carbon emissions. It is pivotal to design an efficient prediction technology to reduce VM energy consumption from the service provider’s consideration while ensuring QoS \cite{chua2024building}. 

Traditional VM energy consumption predicting primarily relied on conventional statistical models, such as the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) \cite{contreras2003arima}, which assumes a linear relationship between historical and future data and models CPU utilization as a linear trajectory. While ARIMA offers exceptional computational efficiency and minimal hardware overhead, it is fundamentally constrained by its linear assumptions. Therefore, classical machine learning methods such as Support Vector Regression (SVR) \cite{bertossi1999fault} and Random Forest(RF) \cite{sheykhmousa2020support} are introduced. SVR utilizes kernel functions to map low-dimensional inputs to high-dimensional feature spaces, enabling recognition of complex patterns. Conversely, RF employs ensembles of decision trees to simultaneously handle heterogeneous resource features. Despite enhanced flexibility, these approaches remain heavily reliant on manual feature engineering. Moreover, in the face of an extremely large amount of constantly changing data, the time dependence of machine learning methods in obtaining past time data and present data are very poor \cite{zhang2021new}. The advent of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) marked a shift toward automated feature extraction. However, RNNs suffer from the vanishing gradient problem, which limits their long-term memory capabilities. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) \cite{karim2017lstm} networks mitigate this issue by introducing gate mechanisms that selectively retain or discard information.

To reduce VM energy consumption, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) \cite{wu2024hotspot} are proposed. By employing dilated convolutions, TCNs achieve extended receptive fields and support parallel processing. However, TCNs assign equal importance to all historical data points, lacking the focusing capability required to distinguish critical events from routine fluctuations.

The above studies have a certain role in improving throughput and achieving automatic feature extraction, but they seldom consider the impact of the VM energy consumption in combination with focusing capability.

\subsection{Research on VM Scheduling}

Effective VM scheduling is the critical phase following energy consumption predicting. Its purpose is to map a large number of VMs to PMs in a manner that minimizes energy consumption while mitigating SLA violations.

In the nascent stages of cloud computing, VM scheduling strategies primarily relied on simple heuristic algorithms such as First Fit (FF) \cite{wan2010first}. The FF algorithm sequentially scans PMs and assigns VMs to the first node with sufficient remaining capacity. While computationally efficient, FF is inherently non-adaptive. This strategy frequently leads to severe resource fragmentation, where the idle capacity accumulated across multiple PMs is insufficient to accommodate larger tasks. This necessitates activating additional nodes and results in persistently high VM energy consumption.

To overcome the limitations of local search, swarm intelligence algorithms inspired by nature are widely adopted. For instance, Particle swarm optimization (PSO) \cite{ishaque2012improved} mimics the foraging patterns of bird flocks, where particles adjust their trajectories based. While PSO exhibits rapid convergence, it is prone to convergence in high-dimensional VM scheduling problems, where the leader may stagnate at suboptimal solutions, causing the entire swarm to deviate.

In recent years, the Sand Cat Swarm Optimization (SCSO) algorithm has emerged as a prominent meta-heuristic approach, being widely applied across various complex practical engineering and scientific computing scenarios due to its distinct advantages, such as streamlined parameter configuration, rapid convergence, and robust optimization capabilities. For instance, in the domains of cloud computing and Internet of Things (IoT) resource management, SCSO has been successfully utilized for VM placement and large-scale task scheduling to effectively minimize data center energy consumption and enhance Quality of Service (QoS) \cite{li2025workspace}. The biomimetic inspiration for the Sand Cat Swarm Optimization (SCSO) algorithm originates from the unique survival and hunting instincts of the sand cat in nature. Long adapted to extremely arid desert environments, sand cats have evolved a highly acute low-frequency auditory system capable of precisely pinpointing prey hidden beneath the sand. In the mathematical model of the algorithm, this hunting behavior is highly abstracted into two alternating core phases: global exploration and local exploitation.
To control the dynamic transition between these two phases, the algorithm introduces an auditory sensitivity parameter, $r_G$, which decreases linearly with the number of iterations (typically decaying from a maximum constant $S_M = 2$ to $0$). Combined with a random vector $r$, the algorithm defines a nonlinear control factor $R = 2 \times r \times r_G - r_G$. When $|R| > 1$, the sand cats are in the prey-searching phase, where individuals rely on their heightened sensitivity to perform extensive random walks across the entire solution space, thereby effectively preventing the algorithm from being trapped in local optima. Conversely, when $|R| \le 1$, the sand cat confirms the prey's location and enters the attack phase. During this stage, the individual employs a random angle $\theta$ to simulate a multi-directional circular approach (i.e., by introducing $\cos(\theta)$), executing precise local exploitation towards the currently recorded global optimal solution. SCSO simulates the hunting process of felines in arid environments. However, when applied to large-scale computing clusters, SCSO frequently encounters population diversity stagnation. To address this issue, we introduced Lens Imaging Learning (LIL) \cite{lu2021lil}. By utilizing optical imaging principles, an opposite solution is generated, forcing the population to look in the opposite direction. This enables the algorithm to escape from the current local optimum and explore unexplored areas, thereby enhancing its ability to find the global optimum. This forces the population to explore uncharted regions of the search space to obtain superior global solutions.

We employ BAK-SCSO-VMS as our forecasting and scheduling framework. By accurately predicting VM energy consumption and scheduling VMs to appropriate PMs, we achieve efficient resource allocation and, while ensuring service quality, reduce overall energy consumption. Figure 1 depicts the overall framework of the BAK-SCSO-VMS system for VM energy  consumption prediction and scheduling. This framework is constituted by two primary blocks: a energy prediction module driven by BiTCN-Attention-KAN (BAK) , and a VM scheduling decision unit based on the upgraded Sand Cat Swarm Optimization (SCSO-LIL). Specifically, the BiTCN-Attention-KAN module first assumes the role of capturing chronological dependencies among the input data to project the power status for the subsequent timeframe, allowing for the preemptive detection of PM overloads. To tackle the cross-layer challenge of translating total PM load predictions into fine-grained VM energy consumption, this paper proposes a two-stage 'decoupling-then-mapping' mechanism. First, in the resource decoupling phase, the model dynamically calculates the resource contribution weight of each VM based on its CPU utilization within a historical observation window. Utilizing these weights, the future total PM load predicted by the BiTCN-Attention-KAN module is accurately decoupled into the expected resource utilization of individual VMs. Subsequently, in the energy mapping phase, considering the additional resource overhead induced by the hypervisor and the highly nonlinear nature of the underlying physical hardware's power consumption, this paper discards traditional static linear power models. Instead, KAN are introduced for deep feature fitting. Leveraging the superior learnable activation functions and nonlinear representation capabilities of KAN, this module adaptively captures the complex mapping relationship between 'expected resource utilization' and 'actual individual VM power consumption'. Consequently, it outputs highly accurate VM-level future energy predictions, providing exceptionally reliable a priori data support for subsequent proactive resource scheduling and energy optimization. Subsequently, the scheduling module applies the modified SCSO-LIL technique to formulate a system-wide optimal VM scheduling strategy derived from the projections of the first module. After carrying out the respective scheduling operations, the overarching global state is finally refreshed. Our framework is responsible for the scheduling of VMs to PMs of the data center based on the prediction and optimization models. We model the cloud data center as a set containing $N$ PMs $H=\{h_{1}, h_{2}, ..., h_{N}\}$.  Considering that many different indicators are involved in practice,  each PM differs in the number of CPU cores,  memory capacity, and bandwidth. The VM set is represented as $V=\{v_{1}, v_{2}, ..., v_{M}\}$.  The objective for $V$ is to find an optimal placement matrix $X$ = \begin{equation}
X = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    X_1 \\
    X_2 \\
    \vdots \\
    X_N
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
    x_{1,1} & x_{1,2} & \cdots & x_{1,D} \\
    x_{2,1} & x_{2,2} & \cdots & x_{2,D} \\
    \vdots  & \vdots  & \ddots & \vdots \\
    x_{N,1} & x_{N,2} & \cdots & x_{N,D}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:population_matrix}
\end{equation} , where $x_{ij}=1$ indicates that VM $v_i$ is placed on PM $h_j$.  This search process is subject to a hard constraint on the quantity of PMs,  that is,  the resource demand of all VMs on a single PM is in the range of the numbers of that PM.

\subsection{PM Energy and SLA Violation Model}

In a cloud computing environment, the energy consumption of PM is not simply summed up. Relevant studies indicates \cite{ilager2020thermal} that the CPU is the part with the largest proportion of energy consumption in the PM, and the relationship between its energy consumption and resource utilization is not a simple linear one. Current models often misjudge the sudden increase in energy consumption under high loads. For example, when dealing with high-load inputs, LSTM exhibits insensitivity to energy consumption fluctuations at load levels exceeding 90\%, leading to misjudgments. Furthermore, ARIMA \cite{contreras2003arima} is inadequate for high-load forecasting, for example, the accuracy of energy consumption predictions for loads between 90\% and 95\% declines much more sharply than it does under low-load conditions. Therefore, the energy consumption prediction model adopted in this study depicts the instantaneous energy $P_i(t)$ of the PM $h_{i}$ at a certain moment as the sum of the reference energy consumption \cite{zhao2018power}\cite{wang2024reconfigurable} in the non-operating state and the energy consumption in the high-load operating state. Specifically, even if the current PM is not overloaded, it still consumes about 70\% of its maximum energy. This is to ensure the normal standby of the PM's hardware such as the motherboard fan, hard drive, and network interface. When the CPU utilization rate increases due to the current load, energy consumption  increases exponentially \cite{zhao2018power}\cite{wang2024reconfigurable}. This also indicates a key solution: if you want to minimize energy consumption, the most direct method is to use integration technology to concentrate VMs on as few PMs as possible, so that more idle PMs can enter sleep or shut down states.The calculation formula of  $P_i(t)$ is as follows:\begin{equation}
    P_i(t) = k \cdot P_{max} + (1-k) \cdot P_{max} \cdot u_i(t)^\gamma,
\label{eq:power_model}
\end{equation}
where $P_i(t)$ represents the real-time energy consumption of the i-th PM at time $t$, $P_{max}$ is the maximum energy when the PM is operating at full capacity, $k$ is the idle energy coefficient, which is the proportion of the PM's energy consumption in the idle state (utilization rate of 0) to its maximum energy consumption, $u_i(t)$ represents the current CPU utilization rate, and $\gamma$ is a nonlinear calibration parameter used to more accurately calculate the nonlinear relationship between load and energy consumption in actual scenarios (if $\gamma=1$, it degenerates to a linear model).

However, relying solely on the energy consumption model (Fig.1) for resource scheduling is insufficient. Specifically, when the PMs becomes overloaded and cannot provide sufficient CPU clock cycles or memory required by the VMs, the lack of underlying resources directly result in the blocking of computational tasks and increased response delays, ultimately leading to Service Level Agreement (SLA) violations. To precisely calculate this potential performance loss while minimizing energy consumption. The SLA violation rate (SLAV) has been established as a key constraint metric, measuring system stability in terms of time. When the CPU utilization rate of a certain physical machines (PMs) reaches 100\%, it indicates that the physical machine is in an overloaded state. SLATAH is defined as the ratio of the time all PMs are in an overloaded state to their running time. This metric reflects the negative effects brought about by integration, that is, the higher the degree of integration, the greater the probability of overload, which leads to a higher SLATAH.\begin{equation}
    SLATAH = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{T_{o,i}}{T_{a,i}},
    \label{eq:slatah}
   \end{equation}
where $N$ represents the total number of active PMs, $T_{o,i}$ is the total time that the $i$-th PM is at 100\% CPU utilization, and $T_{a,i}$ is the total active running time of that PM. The overall formula calculates the average proportion of time that all PMs are unable to provide normal services due to overload.

To eliminate overload situations or integrate resources, the system needs to constantly migrate VMs \cite{fernando2023v}. However, the scheduling process itself is not completely energy-free. Scheduling consumes additional CPU cycles to write dirty pages in memory back to the hard disk. PDM (Performance Degradation due
to Scheduling) \cite{xia2015stochastic} quantifies the overall performance degradation caused by VM scheduling.
\begin{equation}
    PDM = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=1}^{M} \frac{C_{d,j}}{C_{r,j}},
    \label{eq:pdm}
    \end{equation}
where PDM (Performance Degradation due to Scheduling) measures the average percentage of performance degradation caused by virtual machine scheduling, $M$ represents the total number of VMs undergoing scheduling, $C_{d,j}$ is the performance estimation value of the performance loss caused by the $j$-th virtual machine during the scheduling process, and $C_{r,j}$ is the total capacity requested or allocated by that virtual machine. The overall calculation of the formula is the average negative impact of all scheduling operations on virtual machine performance.

    Combining the above two points, the Service violation Rate (SLAV) is defined as the product of SLATAH and PDM:\begin{equation}
    SLAV = SLATAH \times PDM,
    \label{eq:slav}
\end{equation}
This definition indicates that SLAV approaches zero only when both system stability (SLATAH) and scheduling dropout (PDM) are optimized under non-overloaded conditions. 

\subsection{BiTCN-Attention-KAN (BAK) model}

Current resource management models all use a threshold-based, passive triggering mechanism, which triggers scheduling when the server's CPU utilization exceeds 80\%. This kind of post-event handling strategy has a very high lag. It is often only taken after the system has suffered extremely severe congestion or caused very high energy consumption. In order to achieve a shift from passive response to active management, accurately predicting future energy consumption trends is of great significance. However, the energy consumption vector of cloud data centers has a very high degree of instability and volatility. Existing models such as LSTM or TCN, although performing well in handling stationary situations, often fail in prediction due to the linear idleness of their prediction functions when facing sudden energy consumption changes in cloud computing.
For this situation, we present the  BiTCN-Attention-KAN model.

Fig.2 illustrates the architecture of the BiTCN-Attention-KAN  prediction model. Considering the nonlinear characteristics and reliance on past time data during cloud computing, a three-layer core structure has been designed. First, the data is input into the BiTCN layer, which uses a convolutional model to extract the features of the time series to predict future VM energy consumption. Then, the feature vectors enter the Attention layer, where different weights are dynamically assigned to each vector through the attention mechanism to identify and focus on the past time points and nearby time points that have the greatest impact on future loads, such as sudden load points. Finally, the KAN layer is used, and the edge functions within it are utilized for efficient nonlinear mapping to output the final predicted resource demand situation. This end-to-end design significantly enhances the ability of the model in this study to predict energy consumption in complex and variable situations in reality.

\subsection{Sand Cat Swarm Optimization (SCSO) Model}

SCSO \cite{li2025workspace} is a population algorithm that learns the way sand cats use low-frequency noise to find food. This algorithm achieves an adaptive adjustment from carpet-style search to local refined search through the sand cat's auditory sensitivity regulation mechanism. The algorithm introduces a sensitivity parameter $R_g$ which is used to calculate the auditory sensitivity
$R_g$ of the sand cat:\begin{equation}
    R_g = S_m - \left( \frac{S_m \times t}{T_{\max}} \right),
    \label{eq:scso_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
where $S_m$ represents the maximum sensitivity constant, $t$ denotes the current iteration count, and $T_{max}$ signifies the preset maximum total iteration count. As the iteration process progresses (i.e., $t$ gradually increases), this formula causes $R_g$ to linearly decrease from $S_m$ to 0, thereby guiding the algorithm's focus to smoothly transition from wide-area global search in the early stages to local precise exploitation in the later stages.

As the number of iterations continuously increases, $r_G$ gradually decreases, which represents simulating the auditory focus situation of the sand cat when approaching prey.  When $|R| > 1$ (where $R = 2R_g \times \text{rand} - R_g$), the sand cat relies on the current best position and other individuals' positions, take an overall average value, and then move to that average value position before conducting a random search.
    
When $|R| \le 1$, the sand cat approaches the food through its super-sensitive hearing. At this time, it conducts a refined search:
\begin{equation}
    X_{new} = 
    \begin{cases} 
    % 第一种情况：拆分为两行
    \begin{aligned}
        &\frac{X_{best} + X_{centroid}}{2} + \\
        &R_g \cdot (X_{curr} - X_{rand}) 
    \end{aligned} 
    & |R| > 1 \quad \\
  % 增加一点垂直间距
    \\[5pt]   
    % 第二种情况：拆分为两行
    \begin{aligned}
        &X_{best} - R_g \cdot \text{rand} \cdot \\
        &\cos(\theta) \cdot (X_{best} - X_{curr})
    \end{aligned} 
    & |R| \le 1 \quad,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:scso_update}
\end{equation}where  $X_{best}$ represents the current optimal position found by the population, $X_{centroid}$ denotes the average geometric center of all individuals, and $X_{curr}$ signifies the current location of the search agent. The parameter $X_{rand}$ corresponds to a randomly selected position from the population, providing the necessary stochastic diversity to the search process. Additionally, $\text{rand}$ is a random number uniformly distributed in the range $[0, 1]$, and $\cos(\theta)$ acts as a directional factor to facilitate spiral-shaped movement. The variable $R$ serves as a critical switching threshold to balance the exploration and exploitation phases. 

\section{Algorithm Design of the BAK-SCSO-VMS}

\subsection{Energy Prediction by the BiTCN-Attention-KAN model}

Tasks issued by clients are enclosed in one or more VMs, and some hot tasks replicate themselves in multiple VMs to cope with failures. We postulated that a single VM executes a single task, and the VM terminates after the task is executed. It is noteworthy that the task is encapsulated in the VM. Thus, our approach is independent of the type of task. We considered processing tasks by PM in data centers as the allocation of PMs by VMs (e.g. CPU and Memory). To achieve energy reduction, we introduce the Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Network (BiTCN) to capture the temporal correlation of VM energy consumption. BiTCN employs a causal dilation convolution mechanism, ensuring that prediction relies only on past data, while utilizing its expansibility to sample data from relatively distant positions in the input sequence. The output of its residual block is as follows:
\begin{equation}
    H_l(t) = \text{ReLU}\left( \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w_k \cdot H_{l-1}(t - k \cdot d_l) + b \right),
    \label{eq:dilated_conv}
\end{equation}
where $H_l(t)$ and $H_{l-1}$ denote the output vector of the $l$-th layer at time step $t$ and the input sequence of the $(l-1)$-th layer, respectively,  $w_k$ and $b$ represent the weight parameters and bias term of the convolution kernel,  $d_l$ is the dilation factor of the layer determining the sampling interval of the kernel,  $K$ is the kernel size, while BiTCN can extract rich temporal features, its output layer is usually a linear layer, which leads to a decline in the model's processing ability for nonlinear data. Therefore, Kolmogorov-Arnold Network is integrated into BiTCN to enhance performance.

\begin{algorithm}[!t] 
\caption{Training Process of the BiTCN Energy Consumption Prediction Model}
\label{alg:bitcn_training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
% --- 输入输出 ---
\State \textbf{Input:} Historical energy consumption sequence $\mathcal{D}$, Sliding window size $w$, Learning rate $\eta$, Maximum Epochs $K$.
\State \textbf{Output:} Trained neural network parameters $\Theta^*$.

% --- 步骤 1: 初始化 ---
// \textbf{Step 1: Initialization}
\State Initialize network weights $W$ and biases $b$ (Xavier initialization)
\State Preprocess data: Normalize $\mathcal{D}$ to $[0, 1]$ and create sliding windows;

% --- 步骤 2: 训练循环 ---
// \textbf{Step 2: Training Loop}
\While{$k < K$ and not converged}
    \For{each mini-batch $(X_b, Y_b)$ in Training Set}
        \State // \textbf{Forward Propagation}
        \State $F_{fwd} \leftarrow \text{DilatedConv1D}(X_b)$; \Comment{Forward features}
        \State $F_{bwd} \leftarrow \text{DilatedConv1D}(\text{Reverse}(X_b))$; \Comment{Backward features}
        \State $H_{comb} \leftarrow \text{Concat}(F_{fwd}, F_{bwd})$; \Comment{Feature Fusion}
        \State $\hat{Y}_b \leftarrow \text{FullyConnected}(H_{comb})$;
        
        \State // \textbf{Backpropagation}
        \State Compute Loss $\mathcal{L} = \text{MSE}(\hat{Y}_b, Y_b)$;
        \State Calculate gradients $\nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L}$;
        \State Update parameters: $\Theta \leftarrow \Theta - \eta \cdot \text{Adam}(\nabla_\Theta \mathcal{L})$;
    \EndFor
    \State Validate model performance on Validation Set;
    \State $k \leftarrow k + 1$;
\EndWhile

\State \textbf{Return} Optimized parameters $\Theta^*$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

In the feature sequence extracted by BiTCN, not all historical data information has significant reference value and meaning \cite{song2024predicting}. For instance, when predicting the maximum value of a single energy consumption, the weights of adjacent data from the previous few seconds and similar peak data from the past should be much higher than those during the stable period \cite{bi2023network}.

The calculation is defined as follows:\begin{equation}
        \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V,
        \label{eq:attention}
    \end{equation}
where $Q$, $K$, and $V$ represent the current query vector, the indexing features used for retrieval, and the actual content information, respectively,  The context vectors, weighted by the Attention mechanism, are directly fed into the KAN layer to capture intricate inter-data relationships. Derived from the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, KAN places learnable activation functions on the edges (weights) of the network connections. Specifically, each path in the KAN is represented as a variable function rather than a simple scalar weight. During training, these functions adapt their shapes and control points based on the input distribution. In stable phases, the functions approximate linear shapes, however, when encountering anomalies with drastic fluctuations, they transform into complex curves that fit boundaries with high precision. Consequently, the KAN module functions as an adaptive non-linear filter that performs a secondary purification of features extracted by BiTCN. This enhances the model's ability to handle sudden energy peaks, effectively resolving the  peak-shaving, thereby reducing prediction errors in complex non-linear scenarios.

{VM Scheduling based on SCSO-LIL}
The prediction module has already provided the energy consumption status at the future $t+1$ moment, which offers crucial information for the scheduling algorithm. The key point lies in how to formulate the optimal VM scheduling strategy based on these predictive data. We design a Sandcat Swarm Optimization (SCSO) algorithm that integrates Lens Imaging Learning (LIL). Figure 3 depicts the flowchart of the SCSO optimization algorithm incorporating the LIL strategy.\begin{figure}[!t]   % 
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{optimization_flow.png}
    \caption{Flowchart of the SCSO optimization algorithm integrating the LIL strategy}
\end{figure}
The above figure provides a detailed account of the execution process of the improved SCSO algorithm after integrating the LIL strategy. The algorithm first initializes the population and then enters the main loop to simulate the sandcat's perception of the surrounding environment and precise attack when catching prey to update the position of the solution. The decision branch is used to determine whether the current population has become stuck in a local optimum

Although the SCSO algorithm is very good in convergence speed, when processing large-scale discrete data like cloud computing, a situation of converging too prematurely occurs. That is, all individuals quickly focus on a certain local optimal point. This point might have low VM energy consumption but a relatively high SLAV. Due to the lack of a jumping-out mechanism, regarding this situation, this research introduces the Lens Imaging Learning strategy (LIL) from optical imaging, which utilizes the reversible principle of convex lens imaging.As show in Fig.4 for the current optimal solution, it produces a reverse solution. Mathematically, assuming this solution is $X_{best}$, then LIL calculates an $X_{nobest}$ reverse solution without exceeding the current input sequence. This reverse solution is exactly opposite to the optimal solution within the entire solution range. The deep significance of this lies in that, if the current search falls into a local optimal solution, then the opposite area might contain a better solution. LIL uses this method to force the algorithm to look at this reverse solution, thereby allowing the population to jump out of the current local optimal solution and look for the global optimal solution in the blind zone \cite{lu2021lil}. If the reverse solution is better than the current solution, the SCSO-LIL
 algorithm will choose the reverse solution, thereby realizing the escape from the local optimal solution. This mechanism expands the search surface of the population for the input sequence, thereby guaranteeing  the SCSO-LIL algorithm's ability to find the global optimum. The relevant expression and relevant diagram are as follows:\begin{equation}
    X_{nobest} = \frac{lb + ub}{2} + \frac{lb + ub}{2k} - \frac{X_{best}}{k},
    \label{eq:lil_opposition}
\end{equation}where $lb$ and $ub$ represent the lower and upper bounds of the search space respectively, used to determine the geometric center of the search region, $X_{best}$ denotes the position of the current global optimal solution, and $k$ is a scaling factor controlling the perturbation step size. By combining information about the center of the search space with the weighted influence of the optimal solution, this formula forces individuals to move away from the current optimal region, thereby enhancing the algorithm's global search capability.

    \caption{LIL Imaging Principle}
\end{figure}


\begin{algorithm}[!t] 
\caption{Improved SCSO Algorithm with Lens Imaging Learning (LIL)}
\label{alg:scso_lil}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
% --- 输入输出 ---
\State \textbf{Input:} Population size $N$, Max iterations $T_{max}$, Search boundaries $[LB, UB]$.
\State \textbf{Output:} The global best solution $X_{best}$ (Optimal VM Scheduling Strategy).

% --- 步骤 1: 初始化 ---
\State \textbf{Step 1: Initialization}
\State Initialize population $X_i (i=1...N)$ randomly within $[LB, UB]$;
\State Calculate fitness for all agents using Eq. (11) (Energy \& SLA);
\State Identify the current best solution $X_{best}$;

% --- 步骤 2: 主循环 ---
\State \textbf{Step 2: Main Optimization Loop}
\While{$t < T_{max}$}
    \State Update sensitivity range $r_G$ linearly from 2 to 0;
    \For{each search agent $X_i$ in population}
        \State Calculate Energy parameter $E$; \Comment{Controls search behavior}
        
        % 探索与开发
        \If{$|E| \ge 1$} 
            \State \textbf{Exploration Phase:} Update position randomly (Search for prey);
        \Else
            \State \textbf{Exploitation Phase:} Update position towards $X_{best}$ (Attack prey);
        \EndIf
        
        % --- LIL 策略 (核心改进) ---
        \State \textbf{Step 3: Lens Imaging Learning Strategy}
        \State Generate opposition solution $X'_{i}$ using LIL principle;
        \State Calculate fitness of $X'_{i}$;
        \If{$Fitness(X'_{i}) < Fitness(X_{i})$}
            \State Update $X_{i} \leftarrow X'_{i}$; \Comment{Accept better opposition solution}
        \EndIf
        
        \State Boundary check for $X_i$ to ensure validity;
    \EndFor
    
    \State Update global best $X_{best}$ if a better solution is found;
    \State $t \leftarrow t + 1$;
\EndWhile

\State \textbf{Return} $X_{best}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

To strike a balance between SLA and energy consumption, SCSO-LIL optimizes a weighted multi-objective fitness function, which is composed of the weighted sum of the energy consumption (Epred) and the SLA violation rate (SLAVpred) predicted by the BiTCN-Attention-KAN model. Setting weights can reflect the system's preference for different objectives. When the current overall load is relatively low, the energy consumption weight can be appropriately increased to pursue extreme energy saving, while during peak periods, SLA is given a higher weight to ensure stability.

The specific objective function is expressed as follows
\begin{equation}
    \text{Minimize } F = \alpha \cdot E_{total}^{pred} + \beta \cdot SLAV^{pred},
    \label{eq:fitness_function}
\end{equation}
where $E^{pred}_{total}$ and $SLAV^{pred}$ represent the predicted total energy consumption of the data center and the Service Level Agreement violation rate respectively, serving as the two core metrics for evaluating the quality of scheduling schemes, while $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are weighting coefficients used to regulate the relative importance of these two aspects during the optimization process, thereby guiding the algorithm to minimize negative impacts on service quality while reducing energy consumption.

\subsection{Algorithm Implementation Procedures}

Due to the change of time, the initial VM placement with the fluctuation of workload causes part of the PM overload problem, which can lead to SLA violations, further contributing to the rise of overall energy consumption of data centers and QoS degradation. For this reason, we make use of dynamic scheduling algorithm to transfer the VMs of the overloaded physical machine to the PM with currently lower energy consumption, so as to achieve the reduction of overall energy consumption. The BiTCN-Attention-KAN model checks the status of all PMs in the data center against a set maximum energy consumption threshold $EC\_{max}$ to periodically and dynamically migrate VMs to target PMs.

In order to determine the PM energy consumption threshold of PM, we leveraged the methodology of related research, i.e., we take the top 90\% of the PMs with the highest energy consumption as the servers that require moving out of the VMs, which of them is selected the lowest value of the energy consumption as the current maximum PM energy consumption threshold. This scheduling method effectively obtains the physical machines with the lowest energy consumption to house the VMs. Those VMs with the lowest CPU usage among physical machines that exceed the maximum energy consumption threshold can be determined to be migrated. We take advantage of the SCSO-LIL algorithm to move out to the target physical machine, which is less than the maximum energy consumption threshold. In other words, multiple VMs are migrated to the physical machines with the lowest energy consumption.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

Cloudsim 3.0.3 is chosen as the simulation platform for the experiment, mainly because this platform has its own architecture for discrete case simulation. This architecture accurately obtains the subtle changes of VMs during scheduling. Moreover, this platform also supports interfaces that are customized to adjust energy consumption, which is very important for verifying energy consumption optimization in various situations. In reality, cloud loads generally have unpredictable traffic compared to artificially synthesized data. The data contains fluctuating data with periodic changes and randomly occurring energy consumption values that are difficult to predict. This complex situation can test the model's predictive ability in various circumstances. Moreover, before the data is input into the model, it is uniformly processed to reduce the impact of numerical differences caused by different PMs.

Two publicly published real datasets were used to evaluate the effectiveness of the BAK-SCSO-VMS algorithm. They are described below:

\begin{itemize}
\item\textbf{PlanetLab Data \cite{park2006comon}:}This workload trace is provided as part of the CoMon project, a monitoring infrastructure for PlanetLab. In this project, the CPU utilization data of more than a thousand VMs from servers located at more than 500 places around the world are recorded every 5 minutes for 24 hours. During the experiment, we randomly chose the workload trace of 10 days in March and April 2011.

\item \textbf{Google Cluster Data (GCD) \cite{reiss2011google}:} This workload trace was released by Google in May 2011. It contains the usage information of machines in a Google cluster for 29 days. We extracted the task usage table, which contains the resource usage of tasks (e.g., CPU rate, memory usage, etc.) over time. In our experiments, we use the CPU usage data of 1,000 tasks over a 24-hour period.  

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison algorithm}

We intended to compare BAK-SCSO-VMS with the following six compared models and algorithms to verify the superiority of the BAK-SCSO-VMS approach in optimizing data center energy consumption and SLA guarantees.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{First Fit(FF) \cite{wan2010first}:} The default scheduling policy of the Cloudsim platform, which represents a passive response mode without any predictive situations. The performance data of this are used as the baseline model of this experiment to evaluate the effect brought about by the introduction of the prediction model.

\item \textbf{EIM \cite{xu2024advancements}:} EIM is an algorithm based on a single-hidden layer feedforward neural network. It randomly initializes input layer weights and only analytically solves for the output layer weights, thus possessing extremely fast training speed. However, due to the randomness of its parameter initialization, the model often lacks generalization ability and prediction stability when facing cloud data center workloads with high nonlinearity and volatility.

\textbf{Vanilla-RNN \cite{rahhal2020iot}:} Vanilla-RNN is a recurrent neural network for processing sequential data, which passes the hidden state of the previous time step to the current time step through recurrent units, thereby capturing temporal dependencies. However, due to its relatively simple network structure, it is prone to encountering vanishing gradient or exploding gradient problems during the training process, leading to difficulties in effectively capturing long-term dependencies in long sequence workloads and limiting prediction accuracy.

\item \textbf{LSTM \cite{karim2017lstm}:} LSTM is a variant designed to solve the RNN vanishing gradient problem. It introduces a gating mechanism (input gate, forget gate, and output gate) to regulate the information flow, thereby enabling it to effectively maintain and forget information in long sequences. Although LSTM performs excellently in capturing long-term dependencies, its complex internal structure leads to high computational overhead, and its serial processing manner limits parallel computing capability and training efficiency.

\item \textbf{TCN\cite{li2023tcn}:} TCN is an architecture based on causal dilated convolution, combining the parallel computing advantages of convolutional neural networks with sequence modeling capabilities. By stacking dilated convolution layers and residual connections, TCN is able to obtain an extremely large receptive field with fewer parameters, thereby effectively capturing local sudden changes and global trends of workload data, and avoiding the vanishing gradient problem of RNN.

\item \textbf{BiTCN \cite{wei2020bitcoin}:} BiTCN introduces a bi-directional structure on the basis of TCN, extracting temporal features from both forward and backward dimensions simultaneously. This mechanism allows the model not only to utilize past historical workload information but also to combine the overall contextual background of the sequence. Compared with unidirectional models, BiTCN can more comprehensively extract deep features under complex workload patterns, thereby further improving the prediction robustness of the model for high loads and burst traffic.

\end{itemize}
The evaluation indicators selected are some major issues that the data center pays attention to during operation: energy consumption, SLAV and the number of schedulings. Energy consumption is directly related to the cost during operation, while the number of schedulings represents the accuracy of the prediction results, and SLAV is an indicator that can directly reflect the user experience. The ability to maintain a low SLAV while reducing energy consumption is a key criterion for evaluating whether an algorithm is good or not.

\begin{figure}  % <--- 改成 [H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{google_energy_consumption.png}
    \caption{Google Cluster Data Comparison of total energy consumption under different algorithms}
    \label{fig:google Data energy}
\end{figure}
It can be seen from the figure that the reason for the high energy consumption of 452KWh of FirstFit lies in the excessive integration strategy. Due to the lack of prediction for future loads, the FirstFit algorithm tends to keep many PMs in standby mode to ensure timely response when requests arrive. According to the energy consumption model, even if the PM is in an idle state, the energy consumption still reaches about 70\% of the maximum value. This ineffective large number of hole piles is the key reason why the energy consumption remains at a high level and cannot be reduced.
Relatively speaking, the method of this study can reduce energy consumption to 285KWh. The key to this lies in the early perception of the drawbacks caused by the overly integrated strategy. Thanks to the accurate prediction of the BiTCN-Attention-KAN model, the system is able to promptly identify the PMs that may be under low load for a long time in the future. The SCSO-LIL scheduling strategy performs scheduling operations based on this prediction, resolutely migrating VMs from the PM, thereby enabling more physical machines (PMs) to enter sleep mode in a timely manner.

Even the BiTCN model (316KWh), which also has predictive capabilities, still consumes about 10\% more energy than the method in this study. This also reflects from the side the difference in the ability to predict data accuracy. When the BiTCN model is confronted with nonlinear load energy consumption curves, the prediction results usually tend to adopt very conservative strategies, which leads the scheduling algorithm to be reluctant to reduce resources to the extreme. After integrating Attention-KAN, the model has a stronger ability to approximate some more complex curves, and can more accurately improve the smoothness of the curves, thereby giving the scheduling algorithm confidence to further migrate VMs when dealing with fluctuating curves to reduce energy consumption.
\subsection{Analysis of Scheduling Frequency}
\begin{figure}   % <--- 改成 [H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig_migration.png}
    \caption{PlanetLab Data Comparison of cumulative VM schedulings for each algorithm}
    \label{fig:PlanetLab Data scheduling}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure} % <--- 改成 [H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{google_vm_migration.png}
    \caption{Google Cluster Data Comparison of cumulative VM schedulings for each algorithm}
    \label{fig:google Data scheduling}
\end{figure}
As can be seen from the figure, the First Fit algorithm migrates VMs up to 47,281 times, and the number of schedulings is related to the stability of the system when facing dynamic loads. The fewer schedulings there are, the stronger the stability of the system is. However, the number of schedulings of First Fit is too high, reflecting a constantly swinging situation when there is no predictive model. Due to the lack of a predictive model to assist, the algorithm is affected by sudden load fluctuations. For instance, if the energy consumption is high at one moment, the VM is migrated out, and if the energy consumption is low at the next moment, the VM is migrated back. Such frequent scheduling operations lead to a further increase in the energy consumption of the PM.

The method of this study reduced the number of schedulings to 21,832, mainly thanks to optimizations in two aspects. First, the prediction results of the BiTCN-Attention-KAN model filter out some results. For instance, when the load fluctuates for a very short period of time, the model determine whether this is a continuous upward or downward change by combining previous data. If it is judged not to be so, it is ignored. This reduces the excessive scheduling of the scheduling algorithm, thereby avoiding unnecessary schedulings. Secondly, the SCSO-LIL optimization algorithm adopts a global aspect search strategy. Unlike the common greedy strategy, SCSO-LIL considers the algorithm from multiple perspectives, thereby seeking a scheduling strategy that can remain stable for a long time in the future. This one-step VM scheduling method directly avoids the situation where a secondary scheduling is required due to resource competition later on. This reflects the key idea of this study, which is to reduce the frequency of operations by upgrading the quality of decisions.
\subsection{SLAV Analysis}
\begin{figure}[H] 
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig_sla.png}
    \caption{PlanetLab Data SLA Violation Rate Comparison}
    \label{fig:PlanetLab Data SLA}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H] 
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{google_sla_violation.png}
    \caption{Google Cluster Data SLA Violation Rate Comparison}
    \label{fig:google Data SLA}
\end{figure}
As can be seen from the figure, the SLAV has decreased from 0.0276 \% to 0.0165 \%. This improvement reflects the reliability of the system under extreme conditions. The high default rate of First Fit is caused by the random allocation of resources. If the FirstFit algorithm blindly pursues high integration, it strives to fill up the PM. However, if multiple VMs simultaneously request resources at the same time, it causes the PM resources to be consumed all at once, resulting in multiple requests having to be queued, which in turn leads to a breach of SLA.

This method ensures the lowest energy consumption while also guaranteeing the lowest default rate. This may seem contradictory, but in fact, it is all thanks to the strategy of early scheduling. Attention-KAN plays an important role in it. It can accurately identify the key time series where the load changes suddenly. Before the actual load peak arrives, the system has already completed the policy provision in advance. According to this strategy, the SCSO-LIL scheduling algorithm migrates some critical tasks in advance from some PMs with high load risks to some nodes with low energy consumption. This preventive strategy in advance can effectively avoid the occurrence of resource congestion.

In addition, the low default rate of 0.0165 \% also simultaneously demonstrates the advantages of this algorithm. Even in the face of a simulated real environment like PlanetLab, which contains a lot of uncertain and unpredictable data, this method can accurately find the optimal balance point between low energy consumption and low default rate. Experimental data show that the  model successfully solves the problem of being unable to take into account both energy consumption and SLA simultaneously by combining the Attention-KAN and SCSO-LIL algorithms, which have a higher approximation ability for nonlinear curves.

\section{Conclusion}

We proposes a  active resource management framework based on deep learning prediction and group scheduling to address the contradiction between excessive energy consumption and service level agreement (SLA) default rate that may occur in cloud data centers when dealing with dynamic loads related to cloud computing. This framework achieves high-precision prediction of complex nonlinear energy consumption through the BiTCN-Attention  model, thereby overcoming the linear output limitation of the LSTM model in the face of sudden changes in energy consumption. Moreover, it combines the lens imaging Learning (LIL) strategy to improve the Sandcat swarm optimization algorithm (SCSO). The problem of easily getting stuck in local optima when placing a large number of virtual machines has been solved.
